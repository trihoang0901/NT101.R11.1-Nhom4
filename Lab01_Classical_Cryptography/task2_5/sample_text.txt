Classical cryptography studies ways to hide a written message before modern computers existed. A cipher turns readable plaintext into ciphertext. The receiver applies a secret key and recovers the original text. A substitution cipher replaces letters, while a transposition cipher keeps the letters and changes their order. The Caesar cipher shifts every letter by one fixed amount. The Vigenere cipher repeats a keyword, so one plaintext letter can become several ciphertext letters. That property makes a single frequency count less effective than it is against a monoalphabetic cipher. Students still practice these systems because the same ideas return in network security: keys, confusion, diffusion, and the rule that the method may be public while the key stays secret.
